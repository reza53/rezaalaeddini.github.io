Forensic dioramas, most famously known as the Nutshell Studies of Unexplained Death, are a series of intricate, dollhouse-scale crime scenes created in the 1940s by Frances Glessner Lee. 
Known as the "Mother of Forensic Science," she designed these models to train investigators on how to observe scenes and identify vital evidence.

Building a diorama allows students to transform everyday household items, recycled goods, and basic crafting supplies into realistic miniature worlds. 
The process begins with the outer shell, where students typically repurpose a sturdy cardboard shoebox, a shipping box, or pieces of rigid foam board to establish the floor and walls of the scene. 
To texture these surfaces, they can glue down coarse sandpaper to replicate asphalt or dirt, lay down fabric scraps and felt for carpets, or align popsicle sticks and coffee stirrers side-by-side to create convincing hardwood flooring. 
Thin cardstock and patterned scrapbook paper work perfectly as instant wallpaper or painted interior backdrops.

The Training Objective: The dioramas are not meant to be simple puzzles. 
Instead, they were designed to train investigators to overcome unconscious biases and notice the minute, seemingly insignificant details that stand out at crime scenes.
